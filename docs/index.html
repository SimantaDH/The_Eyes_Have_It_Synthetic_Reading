A synthetic reading of sight, the other senses and the performance of seeing in Ruskin Bond's short story

Simanta Nandi, October 2026

Abstract

This project applies synthetic reading (Ghosh, Shanmugapriya T and Mandal, 2026), a computational literary method in which interpretation comes before computation, to Ruskin Bond's "The Eyes Have It". In the story, a blind young man shares a train compartment with a girl and tries to hide that he cannot see. Only at the end does he learn that she was blind too.

A partial reading of 21 of the story's 99 sentences produced the initial impression: two blind travellers perform sightedness for each other, while their real knowledge comes through the other senses. The impression was turned into a prompt with four dimensions: sight, the other senses, performance and imagination. A local language model, run through Ollama, extracted phrases along these dimensions, and every phrase was verified against the text. A researcher-annotated lexicon of 95 phrases was then counted across 20 narrative segments, and the result was replicated with the model's own extraction.

The vocabulary of sight forms three clusters: the declaration of blindness, the revelation, and, largest of all, the narrator's pretence of seeing. Sight in this story is above all something the characters say. The other senses carry what the narrator actually knows of the girl, and imagination fills the stretches where sight is absent. Read through Goffman, Merleau-Ponty, Jay, Garland-Thomson and Mitchell and Snyder, these patterns suggest that the closing irony depends less on blindness than on the social performance of sight, and on the sighted reader's assumption that the girl could see.

Objectives

  • To apply the seven steps of synthetic reading to a short story in Indian English, keeping every interpretive decision human and documented.
  • To trace how the vocabulary of sight, of the other senses, of performance and of imagination is distributed across the narrative.
  • To test a falsifiable statement drawn from the partial reading, rather than to illustrate a conclusion already reached.
  • To confine the language model to extraction, verify every extracted phrase against the text, and replicate the main figure with the model's output alone.
  • To interpret the patterns through disability studies, phenomenology and the cultural history of vision.

Research questions

How does "The Eyes Have It" distribute the vocabulary of sight, of the other senses, of performance and of imagination across its narrative? And does its closing irony depend on sight itself, or on the social performance of sight?

  1. Where is the vocabulary of sight concentrated, and where is it absent?
  2. Where does the narrator's knowledge of the girl come from: words of sight, or words of the other senses?
  3. Does the vocabulary of performance coincide with the vocabulary of sight, so that sight appears as a performance?
  4. What fills the narrative where sight is absent?

Statement tested, written before the full reading

S1
Sight vocabulary will cluster at the two ends of the story: the declaration of blindness and the closing revelation.
S2
The middle of the story will be dominated by the vocabulary of performance: the pretence at the window and the flattery.
S3
The narrator's knowledge of the girl will come through the other senses rather than through sight.

The statement would be falsified if sight vocabulary were spread evenly through the story, if performance vocabulary were absent from the middle, or if the other senses were absent where the narrator learns about the girl.

The story and the corpus

A young man who is blind travels alone in a train compartment towards Dehra. At Rohana a girl gets in. Because he cannot see her, he learns about her through sound, and he decides not to let her discover that he is blind. They talk about the hills of Mussoorie, the landscape outside the window and her face. At Saharanpur she leaves, a new passenger enters, and the last lines change the meaning of everything before them.

The corpus was prepared from a web transcription of the story. Everything outside the story was removed: the site header, blog metadata, the author signature and all that follows it, an image caption, and empty lines. The text was not lower-cased, stemmed or stripped of stop words, because every extracted phrase is checked against the wording as published.

Method

Synthetic reading moves from a situated human impression to computation and back to interpretation. The seven steps followed here are those of the reference study, carried out in a single Google Colab notebook.

  1. Partial reading

    The 99 sentences were divided into three equal parts, and 20% of each part was read: the first seven sentences, seven chosen at random from the middle (seed 42), and the last seven.

  2. Initial impression

    The impression, the research question, the conditions that would falsify the statement, and a positionality statement were written before the full story was read closely.

  3. Text upload

    The cleaned story was numbered sentence by sentence and given to the language model in full.

  4. Query generation

    The prompt turns the impression into four dimensions and asks for verbatim phrases of 1–8 words, each with its sentence number, in a fixed JSON schema. Interpretation is explicitly excluded.

  5. Targeted extraction

    A local open-weight model, run through Ollama, extracted the phrases. Each phrase was checked against the story and against the sentence the model named. Then the researcher assigned the lexicon's phrases to three stages defined by the story's turning points.

  6. Computation and visualisation

    The phrases were counted across 20 equal narrative segments of 4–5 sentences each, with the longest match counted first, and drawn as eight figures. The analysis was repeated with 10 segments.

  7. Interpretation

    The patterns were read through literary and cultural theory and checked against a close reading of the full text.

What the lines suggested

The narrator names his blindness at once, as "totally blind", "sensitive only to light and darkness". His first knowledge of the girl is a sound: her slippers "slapped against her heels". In the middle he fears discovery ("Then I made a mistake"), "felt for the window ledge" and made "a pretence of studying the landscape". He compliments a face he cannot see. In the closing lines a sighted man says "It was her eyes I noticed, not her hair", and reveals that she was "completely blind". The impression that followed: two blind travellers each perform sightedness for the other, and the irony may rest on the performance of sight rather than on sight itself.

Positionality

The researcher reads as a sighted student of literature in India. That fact matters most here: the story's twist is designed for sighted readers, who share the assumption it exposes. To guard against finding only what was expected, the falsification conditions were written in advance, every machine-extracted phrase was verified, and the researcher's lexicon was compared with the model's extraction.

Discussion

The statement, tested

S1, partly supported
Sight clusters at the opening (segments 1–3) and at the end (17–20), but its largest cluster is in the middle (8–12), where the narrator is pretending to see.
S2, supported
Performance vocabulary peaks in segments 9–12, with 10 of its 21 hits, during the window scene and the flattery.
S3, supported
The other senses dominate where the narrator learns about the girl: her slippers and voice at the start, and her voice, perfume and hair at the parting (segments 13–17).

Sight as a spoken performance

The middle cluster of sight vocabulary coincides with the peak of performance vocabulary. In "The Eyes Have It", sight is above all something the characters say. The narrator asks what it is like outside, is told to "look out of the window", makes "a pretence of studying the landscape", and compliments "an interesting face". Goffman's account of passing describes this exactly. A person with a discreditable stigma manages information so that others will not discover it, and conversation is where the management happens. Martin Jay's history of ocularcentrism explains why the disguise works: where knowing is equated with seeing, speaking the vocabulary of sight is enough to pass as sighted. The last line shows that the girl was passing too, so the middle of the story is a conversation between two performances.

Knowledge through the other senses

The balance chart shows where knowledge actually comes from. It turns positive whenever the narrator is learning rather than performing: her slippers, the window ledge under his hand, a voice with "the sparkle of a mountain stream", "the perfume from her hair". For Merleau-Ponty, perception is embodied and multisensory, and his blind man's stick, which becomes part of the body, has its counterpart in the hand on the window ledge. Georgina Kleege's critique of sighted assumptions applies too. The narrator's knowledge of the girl is rich and intimate, and it lacks only what a sighted culture counts as knowledge: how she looks.

Imagination in the gaps

Fourteen of the 22 imagination hits (64%) fall in the nine segments that contain no sight vocabulary at all, which make up 45% of the story. Two scenes carry most of them. The hills of Mussoorie are recalled "calling on my memories", with wild dahlias and a log fire: memory standing in for present sight, so that he can join a sighted conversation about scenery. Then he wonders about her hair, worn in a bun or loose over her shoulders: imagination standing in for sight in desire. In this story the mind's eye does the work the eyes cannot.

The revelation and the sighted reader

The final segment is the most purely visual moment of the story: five sight phrases and no other sense at all. It belongs to the only sighted character, and his sight is partial too: "It was her eyes I noticed, not her hair". The twist works only because the reader, like the narrator, has assumed all along that the girl could see. Garland-Thomson calls this unmarked default body the normate. "Didn't you notice?" is addressed to the narrator but reaches the reader. Mitchell and Snyder's idea of narrative prosthesis raises a fair objection: the story uses the girl's blindness as the device of its surprise, and so risks treating disability as a punchline. Yet the vocabulary shows that the surprise exposes sighted assumptions rather than blind deficiency. The blind travellers knew each other's voices and presence better than the sighted man knew the girl.

A gaze without sight

Read with Laura Mulvey's account of the gaze, the flattery scene shows the narrator performing a gaze he cannot exercise. He compliments her face, and offers "Few girls can resist flattery" as a sighted man's commonplace. The girl declines the script ("I'm tired of people telling me I have a pretty face") even as she performs her own sightedness. Here the gaze is a social script that both characters speak, which supports the finding that sight in this story is performance.

Limitations

  • Small numbers. With 99 sentences, most cells hold between 0 and 5 hits. The figures are patterns that provoke interpretation, in Stephen Ramsay's sense of algorithmic criticism. They are not statistical evidence.
  • Circular stages. The stages follow the story's turning points, so the stage figures illustrate the reading. The test rests on the stage-independent dimension counts, and on their replication with the model's extraction.
  • An interpretive lexicon. The boundaries between dimensions are judgements. "Faced the girl" is coded as sight, but it is also performance. The lexicon was drafted with AI assistance from a close reading and checked against the verified extraction.
  • The source. The corpus is a web transcription, not the print edition, and it contains a few punctuation slips. These are recorded, not corrected. They do not affect the phrase counts, but citations should refer to the printed text.
  • What counting cannot see. Tone, irony, and the timing of a twist are invisible to frequency counts. Close reading has to supply them.
  • One model, one run. Another model or run may extract different phrases, which is why every phrase is verified and the comparison with the lexicon is published.
  • Positionality. A sighted reader may under-read what a blind reader would notice first.

Conclusion

Synthetic reading made it possible to move from a 21-line impression to a tested claim about the whole story, while keeping the interpretation human-led and every machine step verifiable. The figures confirmed that performance dominates the middle of the story and that the other senses carry what the narrator knows. They also showed what the impression had not anticipated: sight is most present exactly where it is being faked, and imagination fills the places sight leaves empty. "The Eyes Have It" stages sight less as a faculty than as a language. Its last question, "Didn't you notice?", is finally addressed to the reader who never thought to doubt that the girl could see.
