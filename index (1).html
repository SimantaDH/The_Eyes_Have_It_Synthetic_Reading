A synthetic reading of sight, the other senses and the performance of seeing in Ruskin Bond's short story

Simanta Nandi

MA, Digital Humanities and Social Sciences
Indian Institute of Technology (ISM) Dhanbad

October 2026

Each window is a sentence. The 21 lit windows were the only lines read before the interpretation began.

Abstract

This project applies Synthetic Reading, a computational method of literary analysis developed by Ghosh, Shanmugapriya T and Mandal (2026) in which interpretation comes before computation, to Ruskin Bond's short story "The Eyes Have It". In the story, a young man who is blind shares a train compartment with a girl and takes great care not to reveal his blindness. Only after she has left does he learn, from another passenger, that she was blind as well.

The initial impression was formed after reading only part of the story, 21 of its 99 sentences: two blind travellers perform sightedness for each other, while their true knowledge comes through the senses other than sight. This impression was turned into a prompt with four dimensions: sight, the other senses (hearing, touch and smell), performance and imagination. A local language model, run through Ollama, extracted phrases along these dimensions, and each phrase was checked against the text. A researcher-annotated lexicon of 95 phrases was then counted across 20 narrative segments, and the result was replicated using the model's own extraction.

The vocabulary of sight forms three clusters: the declaration of blindness, the revelation and, largest of all, the narrator's pretence of seeing. In this story, sight is above all something the characters say. The other senses convey what the narrator actually knows about the girl, and imagination fills the gaps where sight is missing. Read through Goffman, Merleau-Ponty, Jay, Garland-Thomson, and Mitchell and Snyder, these patterns suggest that the closing irony depends less on blindness than on the social performance of sight, and on the sighted reader's assumption that the girl could see.

Objectives

  • To apply the seven steps of synthetic reading to a short story in Indian English, ensuring that every interpretive decision remains human and documented.
  • To map how the vocabulary of sight, the other senses, performance and imagination is distributed across the narrative.
  • To test a falsifiable statement derived from the partial reading, rather than to confirm a conclusion already reached.
  • To restrict the language model to extraction, check every phrase it extracts against the text, and replicate the main figure using the model's output alone.
  • To interpret the patterns through disability studies, phenomenology and the cultural history of vision.

Research questions

How does "The Eyes Have It" distribute the vocabulary of sight, the other senses, performance and imagination across its narrative? Does its closing irony depend on sight itself, or on the social performance of sight?

  1. Where is the vocabulary of sight concentrated, and where is it absent?
  2. Does the narrator's knowledge of the girl come from words of sight or from words of the other senses?
  3. Does the vocabulary of performance coincide with the vocabulary of sight, suggesting that sight is itself a performance?
  4. Where there is no sight, what fills the narrative?

The statement tested, written before the full reading

S1
Sight vocabulary will be concentrated at the beginning and end of the story: at the beginning with the announcement of blindness, and at the end with the revelation.
S2
In the middle of the story, the vocabulary of performance will dominate: the pretence at the window and the flattery.
S3
The narrator will learn about the girl through the other senses rather than through sight.

The statement would be falsified if sight vocabulary were evenly distributed throughout the story, if performance vocabulary were absent from the middle, or if the other senses were absent where the narrator learns about the girl.

The story and the corpus

A blind young man is travelling alone towards Dehra in a train compartment. At Rohana, a girl gets in. Unable to see her, he learns about her through sound, and he does not want her to know that he cannot see. They talk about the hills of Mussoorie, the countryside outside the window and her face. She leaves at Saharanpur, a new passenger enters, and the last lines change the meaning of everything before them.

The corpus was prepared from a web transcription of the story. Everything on the page except the story itself was removed: the site header, the blog metadata, an image caption, the author's signature and everything that follows it, and empty lines. The text was not lower-cased, stemmed or stripped of stop words, because every extracted phrase is checked against the wording of the text as published.

Method

Synthetic reading moves from a situated human impression to computation and back to interpretation. The seven steps followed here are those of the reference study, carried out in a single Google Colab notebook.

  1. Partial reading

    The 99 sentences were divided into three equal parts of 33, and 20% of each part (seven sentences) was read: the first seven sentences, seven chosen at random from the middle (seed 42) and the last seven, making 21 in all.

  2. Initial impression

    Before the full story was read closely, the impression, the research question and the conditions that would falsify the statement were written down, along with a positionality statement.

  3. Text upload

    After cleaning, the complete story was numbered sentence by sentence and presented to the language model.

  4. Query generation

    The prompt turns the impression into four dimensions and asks for verbatim phrases of one to eight words in each dimension, each with its sentence number, in a fixed JSON schema. Interpretation is explicitly excluded.

  5. Targeted extraction

    The phrases were extracted by a local open-weight model run through Ollama. Every phrase was checked against the story and against the sentence the model identified. The researcher then assigned the phrases of the lexicon to three stages, according to the turning points of the story.

  6. Computation and visualisation

    The phrases were counted in each of 20 equal narrative segments of four to five sentences, with the longest match counted first, and the results were drawn as eight figures. The analysis was repeated with 10 segments.

  7. Interpretation

    The patterns were scrutinised through literary and cultural theory and cross-checked against a close reading of the full text.

What the lines imply

Right away, the narrator names his blindness: he is "totally blind", "sensitive only to light and darkness". His first knowledge of the girl is a sound: her slippers "slapped against her heels". In the middle, he is afraid of being found out ("Then I made a mistake"), "felt for the window ledge" and made "a pretence of studying the landscape". He compliments a face he cannot see. At the end, a sighted man says, "It was her eyes I noticed, not her hair", and reveals that she was "completely blind". The impression that followed: each of the two blind travellers performs sightedness for the other, and the irony may lie in the performance of sight rather than in sight itself.

Positionality

The researcher reads as a sighted student of literature in India. This matters here: the story's twist is designed for sighted readers, who share the assumption it exposes. To avoid finding only what was expected, the conditions of falsification were set in advance, every phrase extracted by the machine was checked, and the researcher's lexicon was compared with the machine's extraction.

Discussion

Supported S2
Performance vocabulary peaks in segments 9–12, the window scene and the flattery, which hold 10 of its 21 hits.
Supported S3
The other senses prevail where the narrator learns about the girl: when she arrives (segments 2–4) and again as she leaves (segments 13–17), from her slippers at the beginning to her voice, perfume and hair at the end.

Sight as an oral performance

The middle cluster of sight vocabulary coincides closely with the peak of performance vocabulary. In "The Eyes Have It", sight is above all something the characters say. The narrator asks what it is like outside, is told to "look out of the window", makes "a pretence of studying the landscape" and compliments "an interesting face". This is exactly what Goffman describes as passing: a person with a discreditable stigma controls information so that others will not discover it, and conversation is where that control takes place. Read in the context of Martin Jay's history of ocularcentrism, in which knowing is equated with seeing, the disguise makes sense: anyone who speaks the language of sight can pass as sighted. The last line reveals that the girl was passing too, so the middle of the story is a conversation between two performances.

Knowledge through the other senses

The balance chart shows where knowledge comes from. It turns positive whenever the narrator is learning rather than performing: her slippers, the window ledge under his hand, a voice with "the sparkle of a mountain stream", "the perfume from her hair". For Merleau-Ponty, perception is embodied, and the blind man's stick becomes part of his body; the hand on the window ledge is its counterpart here. Georgina Kleege's critique of sighted assumptions applies as well. The narrator knows the girl well: her voice, her laugh, her scent, her presence. What he does not know is how she looks, which is precisely what a sighted culture counts as knowledge.

Gaps filled by imagination

Fourteen of the 22 imagination hits (64%) fall in the nine segments that contain no sight vocabulary at all, which make up 45% of the story. Two scenes carry most of them. First, the hills of Mussoorie are recalled "calling on my memories", with wild dahlias and a log fire: memory stands in for present sight, so that he can join a sighted conversation about the scenery. Then he wonders whether she wears her hair in a bun or loose over her shoulders: imagination takes the place of sight in desire. In this story, the mind's eye does the work the eyes cannot.

The revelation and the sighted reader

The final segment is the most purely visual moment of the story: five sight phrases and no other sense at all. It belongs to the only sighted character, and his sight is partial too: "It was her eyes I noticed, not her hair". You too, as a reader, have probably assumed all along that the girl could see; otherwise the twist would not work. Garland-Thomson calls this default, unmarked body the normate. "Didn't you notice?" is asked by the sighted passenger, but it is meant for the reader. Mitchell and Snyder's notion of narrative prosthesis raises a reasonable critique: the story may use the girl's disability as a punchline. Yet the surprise exposes the reader's unspoken sighted assumptions rather than any deficiency in the blind characters. The blind travellers knew each other's voices and presence better than the sighted man knew the girl.

A gaze without sight

Read with Laura Mulvey's account of the gaze, the flattery scene shows the narrator performing a gaze he cannot exercise. He compliments her face, and offers "Few girls can resist flattery" as a sighted man's commonplace. The girl declines the script ("I'm tired of people telling me I have a pretty face") even as she performs her own sightedness. Here the gaze is a social script that both characters speak, which supports the finding that sight in this story is performance.

Limitations

  • Small numbers. With 99 sentences, most cells hold between 0 and 5 hits. The figures are patterns that provoke interpretation, in Stephen Ramsay's sense of algorithmic criticism. They are not statistical evidence.
  • Circular stages. The stages follow the story's turning points, so the stage figures illustrate the reading. The test rests on the stage-independent dimension counts and on their replication with the model's extraction.
  • An interpretive lexicon. The boundaries between dimensions are judgements. "Faced the girl" is coded as sight, but it is also performance. The lexicon was drafted from a close reading and checked against the verified extraction.
  • The source. The corpus is a web transcription, not the print edition, and it contains a few punctuation slips. These are recorded, not corrected. They do not affect the phrase counts, but citations should refer to the printed text.
  • What counting cannot see. Tone, irony and the timing of a twist are invisible to frequency counts. Close reading has to supply them.
  • One model, one run. Another model or run may extract different phrases, which is why every phrase is verified and the comparison with the lexicon is published.
  • Positionality. A sighted reader may under-read what a blind reader would notice first.

Conclusion

Synthetic reading made it possible to move from a 21-line impression to a tested claim about the whole story, while keeping the interpretation human-led and every machine step verifiable. The figures confirmed that performance dominates the middle of the story and that the other senses carry what the narrator knows. They also showed what the impression had not anticipated: sight is most present exactly where it is being faked, and imagination fills the places that sight leaves empty. "The Eyes Have It" stages sight less as a faculty than as a language. Its last question, "Didn't you notice?", is finally addressed to the reader who never thought to doubt that the girl could see.
